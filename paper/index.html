Three Kinds of System Prompt: Comparing What AI Labs Publish About How Their Models Behave

Syed Abdul Aman
Independent researcher · amanbaba9404522@gmail.com
9 pages July 2026, revised October 2026 cs.CL · cs.AI

Abstract

AI labs now publish long documents meant to shape how their models behave. Some are built into the model during training. Some set rules for whose instructions win at runtime. Some are the literal text a product sends with every conversation. People compare these as if they were one kind of thing. I argue they are three, and that mixing them up produces comparisons that don't hold up.

Prior work studies task prompts for benchmarking, attacks that extract system prompts, and how researchers and policymakers discuss system-level instructions. None of it codes the documents themselves. I read the primary documents from Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind and xAI, plus system prompts from more than 25 coding-agent products, tiered each source by how far it can be trusted, and coded each one on seven axes. Four things stood out. The labs don't publish the same kind of document. xAI committed to publishing its prompts in response to an incident. Coding agents spend their prompts on tool use and say comparatively little about content safety. And Anthropic's public changelog for its claude.ai prompt, 18 dated revisions over 23 months, shows that at least one lab's "living document" claim can be checked against a dated record.

One person coded the corpus, so I offer this as a first map to be checked and extended, not a settled result.
