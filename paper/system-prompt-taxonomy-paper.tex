\begin{abstract}
AI labs now publish long documents meant to shape how their models behave.
Some are built into the model during training. Some set rules for whose
instructions win at runtime. Some are the literal text a product sends with
every conversation. People compare these as if they were one kind of thing.
I argue they are three, and that mixing them up produces comparisons that
don't hold up.

Prior work studies task prompts for benchmarking, attacks that extract system
prompts \citep{promptleak2026}, and how researchers and policymakers discuss
system-level instructions \citep{neumann2026governance}. None of it codes the
documents themselves. I read the primary documents from Anthropic, OpenAI,
Google DeepMind and xAI, plus system prompts from more than 25 coding-agent
products, tiered each source by how far it can be trusted, and coded each one
on seven axes. Four things stood out. The labs don't publish the same kind of
document. xAI committed to publishing its prompts in response to an incident.
Coding agents spend their prompts on tool use and say comparatively little
about content safety. And Anthropic's public changelog for its claude.ai
prompt, 18 dated revisions over 23 months, shows that at least one lab's
``living document'' claim can be checked against a dated record.

One person coded the corpus, so I offer this as a first map to be checked and
extended, not a settled result.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} system prompts, LLM alignment, AI governance,
prompt engineering, behavioral control, transparency

\section{Introduction}

Every deployed chatbot runs under instructions written by the company behind
it. These sit above whatever the user types, and people usually call them
``system prompts.'' They used to be a few lines. The documents that shape
model behavior are now long: Anthropic's constitution for Claude runs to
roughly 23,000 words \citep{anthropic2026constitution}, and OpenAI's Model
Spec is 128 pages in the revision I analyze
\citep{openai2025modelspec}.\footnote{I analyze the 2025-09-12 revision
throughout. OpenAI has kept revising the Model Spec since, with updates
through at least March 2026. As with every artifact in this study, I report a
dated snapshot, not a live copy; Section~\ref{sec:methodology} explains how I
handle snapshots.} A whole ecosystem of public repositories also exists just
to collect these documents, whether released officially or pulled out through
extraction. The largest passed 140,000 GitHub stars at the time of writing
\citep{x1xhlol2026repo}.

For all that attention, few people have compared the documents themselves.
Three nearby literatures exist. The first is prompt engineering, which builds
taxonomies of \emph{task} prompts, the instructions a user or developer writes
to get a particular output, mostly for benchmarking and comparing techniques
\citep{teler2023,liu2024survey}. The second is AI security, which treats
system prompts as a \emph{target}: how adversarial queries can pull them out,
and how to prevent that \citep{promptleak2026,proxyprompt2025}. The third, and
the closest to this paper, is governance studies.
\citet{neumann2026governance} review how researchers and policymakers
\emph{talk about} system-level instructions as a way to govern AI, but they do
not code the primary documents and compare them across labs. So the question
I ask is still open: across labs and product types, what do these documents
contain, how are they built, and what does the variation say about how each
company approaches governance?

Three reasons make this worth doing. For governance, these documents are some
of the few concrete things the public can inspect to see how a lab turns its
stated values into instructions. They are more specific than a blog post and
more current than a research paper. For NLP research, using governance
documents as training or evaluation data (some labs already do this
internally) needs a shared vocabulary for what they contain, and the public
literature doesn't have one yet. For engineers, developers building on these
models often mix up different kinds of document, for example by setting a
leaked \emph{deployed} prompt from one product next to an official
\emph{training-time} document from another. Those two can't be compared
directly.

\subsection*{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item I propose a three-part taxonomy that separates training-time
    constitutions, runtime authority specifications, and deployed
    inference-time system prompts, and I show that comparisons across the
    three go wrong.
  \item I build a source-tiered dataset covering four labs and a corpus of
    coding-agent products, coded on seven axes: identity framing,
    safety and refusal instructions, tool-use governance, tone and persona
    control, output format, injection countermeasures, and recency handling.
  \item I report cross-lab findings: labs differ in whether they publish
    governance documents at all and in which kind; one lab's transparency was
    a reaction to an incident; consumer chat and coding-agent products stress
    different things; and one lab's public, dated changelog (18 revisions in
    23 months) lets me measure publication cadence directly, instead of
    taking its ``living document'' description on trust.
  \item I say what I would study next, and what would make these results
    stronger.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Task-Prompt Taxonomies}
Most prompt-taxonomy work is about structuring task instructions so models
score better on benchmarks. The TELeR taxonomy \citep{teler2023} sorts prompts
by turn structure, expression style, level of detail and role, so that
benchmark results can be reported consistently. Broader surveys
\citep{liu2024survey} catalog techniques such as chain-of-thought, few-shot
examples and retrieval augmentation. A related line catalogs prompt
\emph{defects}, the ways task prompts fail, drawing on software-engineering
and NLP venues \citep{promptdefects2025}. All of it treats the prompt as
something a user or developer writes to get a job done. None of it covers the
instruction layer written by the provider, which is what I study.

\subsection{Prompt Security and Extraction}
A large, active literature treats system prompts as secrets worth protecting.
Early work separated prompt injection from prompt leaking as two kinds of
attack \citep{perez2022}. Later studies measured leakage across many deployed
applications and found rates above 80\% in some large-scale settings
\citep{promptleak2026}, and proposed defenses such as proxy substitution
\citep{proxyprompt2025} and encoding-based hardening
\citep{encodingdefense2026}. These papers study the \emph{event} of
extraction and how well a prompt holds up, not what the prompt says. My work
complements them. Security research asks whether a prompt can be extracted. I
ask what it says once it is available through legitimate or well-established
channels, and what patterns show up across many of them.

\subsection{AI Governance Transparency}
A smaller literature, along with some industry practice, deals with
transparency in AI governance more broadly: model cards, system cards,
published behavioral specifications. OpenAI's Model Spec presents itself as a
transparency artifact that governs the ``chain of command'' between the
provider, operators (the authors of system prompts), and users
\citep{openai2025modelspec}. Anthropic's constitution describes itself as
written mainly \emph{to} the model, favoring precision over public
readability, with public release as a secondary benefit
\citep{anthropic2026constitution}.

The closest prior work is a contemporaneous study by
\citet{neumann2026governance}, published at FAccT 2026. They treat
system-level instructions as a governance object. Their method is a
PRISMA-style systematic review of 287 papers, which yields an eight-category
typology of the \emph{goals} researchers attribute to such instructions
(alignment, stability and auditability, for example), plus a policy analysis
of two cases: US Executive Order 14319 and the EU General-Purpose AI Code of
Practice. Their main finding is that governance frameworks often assume
system-level instructions give stable, predictable control, and the technical
literature does not consistently support that. That is a claim about the
literature and the policy discussion \emph{around} these documents. They
organize their typology by the \emph{stakeholder hierarchy} through which
instructions are issued, a ``prompt stack'' of foundation-model provider,
developer or deployer, and end user.

My study differs in scope and method, and does not contradict theirs. I don't
survey what researchers or policymakers claim. I code the content of the
primary documents and compare them, organized by \emph{artifact type}
(training-time, runtime-authority, deployed), which cuts across their
stakeholder axis. From the sections and reference list available to me when I
wrote this, their corpus uses Anthropic's published deployed system prompts
but does not seem to treat Anthropic's training-time constitution as a
separate kind of artifact. That fits my taxonomy, where the constitution is a
different object from the runtime and deployed layers that their framing
describes best. I see the two papers as complementary. Theirs shows, at the
level of research and policy, that the premise of prompt-based governance is
more contested than people assume. Mine gives a primary-source account of what
the documents behind that premise contain.

\section{A Taxonomy of Behavioral-Control Artifacts}
\label{sec:taxonomy}

Informal comparisons of ``system prompts'' across labs mix up three different
kinds of artifact. I propose the following taxonomy, based on the sources I
examined.

\begin{description}[itemsep=6pt]
  \item[Training-time constitutions.] Documents used during training to shape
    the model's weights, usually through constitutional-AI-style methods where
    the document generates synthetic training signal such as example
    interactions, response rankings and scenario guidance. Anthropic's
    constitution is the clearest public case. Claude is its main audience, it
    is not injected at inference time, and it avoids prescribing rules in
    favor of reasoning the model is meant to absorb
    \citep{anthropic2026constitution}.
  \item[Runtime authority specifications.] Documents that say how a deployed
    model should weigh instructions from different sources at inference
    time. They are not a single prompt themselves. OpenAI's Model Spec is the
    clearest case. It defines a ranked ``principal hierarchy'' (provider,
    operator, user) and explains how conflicts between levels should resolve,
    but it is not the text sent in any one conversation
    \citep{openai2025modelspec}.
  \item[Deployed inference-time system prompts.] The actual text placed at the
    start of a conversation for a given product at a given time. This is the
    artifact most often leaked or extracted and, in xAI's case
    \citep{xaigrokrepo2026}, published in raw form on purpose. It is the most
    short-lived of the three, changing with product updates, and the most
    varied, since one lab can run dozens of different deployed prompts across
    its products.
\end{description}

The taxonomy is more than bookkeeping. Section~\ref{sec:findings} shows that
mixing the types produces comparisons that don't hold up, and that which type
a lab picks for a given constraint says something about how that lab thinks
about governance.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Source Tiering}
The corpus mixes documents of different provenance, so each entry carries a
credibility tier, which I report openly instead of treating every source as
equally authoritative. These tiers measure how far a source can be trusted.
They are separate from the three artifact types in
Section~\ref{sec:taxonomy}.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Tier 1 (Official/primary):} Content published directly by the
    lab that wrote it (for example Anthropic's constitution, OpenAI's Model
    Spec, xAI's raw prompt repository).
  \item \textbf{Tier 2 (Reconstructed, high-consensus):} Content that shows up
    consistently across independent extraction attempts and lives in
    long-running, widely cited public repositories (for example the
    coding-agent prompt collection studied in
    Section~\ref{sec:coding-agents}).
  \item \textbf{Tier 3 (Single-source leaks):} Lower-confidence material, used
    only to note that a pattern may exist and never as a primary data point.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Coding Scheme}
Each entry is coded on seven axes: identity framing, safety and refusal
instructions, tool-use governance, tone and persona control, output format
constraints, injection and jailbreak countermeasures, and recency and
knowledge-cutoff handling. Document length and revision history are not coded
as axes. Where a source gives them (the Model Spec's page count, the
constitution's word count, Anthropic's changelog), they sit in the notes
column of the dataset. I derived the scheme inductively from a first pass over
the corpus instead of fixing it in advance, which is standard practice in
qualitative coding of emerging categories \citep{promptdefects2025}.

\subsection{Copyright and Reproduction Policy}
I don't reproduce system prompt text beyond short quoted fragments where the
exact wording matters for the analysis. Everything else is paraphrase and
structural coding, because I treat these documents as evidence, not as text to
republish.

\subsection{Scope and Corpus}
The corpus (Table~\ref{tab:corpus}) covers four labs at the level of official
or near-official governance documents, plus more than 25 coding-agent products
whose prompts come from a single long-running, widely cited Tier-2 collection
\citep{x1xhlol2026repo}. I use the coding-agent set to describe patterns by
product category, not to make claims about any single product.

\section{Findings}
\label{sec:findings}

\subsection{Labs Publish Different Kinds of Document, Not Just Different Content}
The most basic finding is that labs don't agree on which kind of document
should do the governing. Anthropic's main public artifact is a training-time
document that is never injected at runtime. OpenAI's is a runtime framework
for deciding whose instructions win, which is not itself a prompt. xAI's is
the deployed prompts, published raw, with no public training-time or
arbitration document alongside. Google DeepMind has published nothing in any
of the three categories about its own constraints on Gemini. Its public
documentation is prompting guidance for outside developers, not a statement
about how Gemini is governed. So a comparison of ``Anthropic's system prompt
versus OpenAI's versus Google's'' doesn't line up four instances of one
thing. At best it lines up two comparable ones and two gaps.

\subsection{Same Structure, Different Reasons}
Even with different artifact types, Anthropic and OpenAI use a similar
device: an explicit ranking of whose instructions come first. OpenAI's
principal hierarchy ranks provider, operator and user instructions, and
higher levels win when they conflict \citep{openai2025modelspec}. Anthropic's
constitution sets a priority order of safety, ethics, compliance and
helpfulness. They justify it very differently. OpenAI talks about
steerability and developer control, and compares the structure to function
definitions and arguments in a programming language. Anthropic presents its
version as reasoning the model should absorb, not rules to apply
mechanically, and says it prefers explanation over rule lists because rule
lists don't generalize to new situations. The structure looks alike but the
philosophy is not, and an analysis that only asked ``does the document
specify a hierarchy?'' would miss the difference.

\subsection{Transparency Can Be a Reaction}
Press coverage often describes xAI's publication of Grok's raw system prompts
as a transparency move on par with Anthropic's. Where it came from is
different. xAI announced the commitment in direct response to a May 2025
incident, in which an unauthorized internal change led the Grok bot on X to
give off-topic, ideologically directed replies \citep{xaiincident2025}. It
framed publication as a way to rebuild trust and paired it with more internal
review controls. That is not the principled, forward-looking stance behind
Anthropic's and OpenAI's releases. So ``publishes its system prompts'' covers
at least two things: transparency as a value, and transparency as incident
response. They may deserve different levels of trust as a picture of how the
model normally behaves. One public issue thread on xAI's repository points the
same way. It describes a prompt the model reportedly shared in conversation
that was never found in the repository. I don't try to settle what that
means. I flag it because it shows that Tier-1 status does not guarantee
completeness.

\subsection{Content Differs, Not Just Structure}
Labs also differ in what their constraints actually say, and the differences
match how each company positions its product, not just how it writes. xAI's
published safety-prefix file for its consumer model says there are no added
content restrictions on fictional adult sexual content involving dark or
violent themes unless specified elsewhere. It also tells the model to treat
users as adults and not to moralize about ``edgy'' requests, and it marks
these instructions as the highest priority \citep{xaigrokrepo2026}. That is a
more permissive default for mature fiction than anything publicly documented
by the other labs in this corpus. The same material keeps a narrower,
explicitly named restriction on content involving minors. Broad permissiveness
with one hard, named exception looks like a deliberate product choice, not an
oversight, and the primary source says it plainly enough that I don't have to
infer it.

\subsection{Product Category Predicts What a Prompt Emphasizes}
\label{sec:coding-agents}
Coding-agent products (Cursor, Claude Code, GitHub Copilot, Devin and more
than twenty others, all from one widely cited Tier-2 collection) look
different from consumer chat assistants. Their prompts put heavy weight on
governing tools: the order of tool calls, confirmation before destructive file
operations, diff-format conventions. They say comparatively little about
general content safety. I read this as fitting the three-artifact taxonomy in
Section~\ref{sec:taxonomy}. If the base model already carries general safety
behavior from a training-time layer, the product's deployed prompt has a
narrower job: governing what is specific to that product's task. This fits a
layered split of governance work across the three artifact types. It does not
prove one.

\subsection{Publication Cadence Shows That ``Living Document'' Can Be Checked}
\label{sec:cadence}
The Anthropic constitution and the OpenAI Model Spec both describe themselves as living documents that will be revised. Anthropic is the only lab in this corpus where that claim can be checked against a public, dated record instead of taken on the publisher's word. Its changelog for the deployed \texttt{claude.ai} and mobile system prompt lists 18 dated revisions over about 23 months (July 2024 to June 2026). That is a mean gap of roughly 41 days, under six weeks, between documented changes \citep{anthropic2026sysprompts}. The changelog also says these updates do not carry over to the API. That confirms, without any inference from me, that the deployed prompt belongs to a particular product surface and is not one canonical text shared across everything a lab ships, which matches the taxonomy in Section~\ref{sec:taxonomy}. No comparable public record exists for OpenAI, Google DeepMind or xAI at the time of writing, so I can't compare update cadence across labs. I report this as a single case. It shows that at least one lab's ``living document'' claim can be verified independently, and it shows what verifiable transparency could look like for the others.

\section{Discussion}

Two things follow from the findings. First, ``comparing system prompts'' only
makes sense once you say which artifact type you mean. Any comparison that
crosses types, such as a leaked deployed prompt against an official
training-time document, should say so instead of presenting itself as like for
like. Second, the differences between labs are not just style. Which artifact
type a lab chooses, whether it publishes anything, and the circumstances of
publication (planned, or forced by an incident) all tell you something about
its approach to governance. Public discussion tends to flatten this into one
question, ``does lab X publish its prompts?'', and to treat the answer as a
yes-or-no marker of trustworthiness. It isn't one.

\subsection{Implications for Practitioners}
Developers who build on several providers' APIs, including agent systems that
call more than one model, should know that a product's ``safety behavior'' is
not encoded at the same layer for every provider. A prompt that works under
one product's conventions, such as the typically light safety framing of a
coding agent, shouldn't be assumed to carry over to a different deployment
without checking which layer is supposed to carry that responsibility.

\subsection{Implications for Researchers}
I suggest treating these documents as a legitimate, citable object of NLP and
AI-governance research. The credibility tiers used here are one candidate norm
for handling the mix of official and reconstructed material that this area
will keep having until more labs publish everything proactively.

\section{Limitations}

Several limits affect how much weight the findings can bear. First, the
corpus is not exhaustive. It covers four labs in depth and one aggregated
coding-agent source, not the full range of deployed LLM products. Second,
Tier-2 and Tier-3 material carries less evidentiary weight than Tier-1 by
construction. Conclusions that rest mostly on Tier-2 sources, especially the
coding-agent finding in Section~\ref{sec:coding-agents}, are suggestive until
someone replicates them against official sources, if labs publish any. Third,
most of the artifacts are one or a few snapshots, not time series. Publishers
describe the constitution and the Model Spec as living documents, but only
Anthropic's deployed-prompt changelog (Section~\ref{sec:cadence}) gives
public, dated evidence of revision in practice. Findings on emphasis or
structure for OpenAI, Google DeepMind and xAI may not hold at a later
snapshot, and I have no public basis for guessing how fast they would change.
Fourth, I derived the coding scheme from the corpus and applied it alone. I
did not measure inter-rater reliability, and a second coder would make future
versions stronger. Fifth, the interpretation in
Section~\ref{sec:coding-agents}, that base-model training absorbs general
safety behavior while deployed prompts handle task-specific governance, fits
the pattern I saw but was not tested against a case that could prove it wrong,
such as a coding agent built on a base model with no comparable training-time
safety layer. It is the simplest explanation the public evidence allows, not a
demonstrated cause.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

The documents that govern large language models have grown from informal
instructions into long public artifacts, yet few studies compare their primary
text directly, as opposed to reviewing how they are discussed in the
literature and in policy \citep{neumann2026governance}. I introduced a
three-part taxonomy that separates training-time constitutions, runtime
authority specifications and deployed system prompts, and showed that the
separation clears up comparisons across labs that otherwise don't line up.
Using a source-tiered method across four labs and a broad set of coding-agent
products, I found that labs differ in which kind of artifact they publish at
all, that transparency can be a reaction to an incident, that differences in
content track product positioning and not only writing style, that product
category predicts what a deployed prompt emphasizes, and that at least one
lab's claim to maintain a living governance document can be checked against a
public, dated record.

Future work should add more labs and product types as official material
appears, measure inter-rater reliability on the coding scheme, and follow
these artifacts over time, since their publishers call them living documents.
That would let the field study how behavioral-control design changes, not only
how labs differ at one moment.
